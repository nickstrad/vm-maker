# On writing well

Make the reader's next thought easy. Favor clear meaning over clever phrasing and useful detail
over length. This is an original working guide informed by Zinsser's teaching, not a request to
impersonate him or a substitute for his book. See [sources](references/sources.md) when attribution
or the basis of a rule matters.

## Shape the piece

- Find one central point and the reader's reason to care. Let that point decide what belongs.
  Follow the user's chosen scope and format; do not force every piece into the same outline.
- Start with the concrete question, event, observation, or claim. Supply context before the
  reader needs it. Build each paragraph on what the last one established.
- Keep the details that explain the mechanism or support the claim. Cut detours and repeated
  explanations. End when the point has landed; do not add a ceremonial recap.
- Keep the author's own voice. First person can suit a personal account, but do not invent
  experiences, feelings, dialogue, measurements, or lessons learned to make it sound personal.

## Make each sentence earn its place

- Name the actor and the action when they matter: “PostgreSQL writes the record” is easier to
  follow than “the writing of the record is performed.” Passive voice still fits when the actor
  is unknown or the affected thing is the point.
- Prefer a familiar, exact word to an ornate one: “use” instead of “utilize,” “before” instead
  of “prior to.” Judge words by meaning and reader effort, not their Latin roots. Keep needed
  terms such as transaction, replication, and isolation; explain them where needed.
- Give a sentence one main job. Split it when clauses make the reader hold too much at once.
  Vary the rhythm enough to sound human; no fixed word limit or stream of choppy fragments.
- Replace abstract noun piles with actions. Remove throat-clearing, empty emphasis, stock
  transitions, and explanations the evidence already makes clear.
- Prefer concrete names and quantities to vague praise or dramatic labels. Keep qualifications
  that affect truth: “may,” “in this run,” and “with this setting” are not clutter when they
  define what the evidence supports.

## Revise without changing the facts

Read once for structure, then for sentences. Check that every claim has support and every paragraph
moves the piece forward. Read the result aloud, or simulate that pass, to catch awkward rhythm and
ambiguous pronouns.

For technical prose, preserve identifiers, commands, units, conditions, distinctions, and the
scope of each result. Do not “simplify” a causal claim into a stronger one. Separate recorded
observations from expected behavior and inference. Editing prose alone does not authorize changing
code or running a lab.

Deliver the requested prose, not a report about this checklist. Briefly flag any unresolved fact
that materially affects the draft. Specific user choices about tone, length, and format take
precedence over these defaults.
